How it fits together

A web app, a server, and an optional bridge to your radio.

Browser The dashboard. Runs on anything — Pi kiosk, laptop, tablet, phone. Propagation physics runs here, in WebAssembly.
⇄
Server Talks to the cluster, RBN, POTA, NOAA, NASA, SatNOGS… caches it for everyone. openhamclock.com, or your own Pi / Docker / Mac.
⇄
Rig Bridge Small program on the PC by the radio. CAT control, WSJT-X, rotator, APRS TNC, sensors. Optional.
  • Hosted: openhamclock.com — nothing to install, thousands of operators use it daily
  • Self-hosted: same code, your LAN, your data; every upstream call proxied through your own server
  • Our own DX cluster node feeds the hosted site — RBN skimmers plus human spots, mode-balanced
  • No accounts, no tracking; settings live in your browser (with optional sync and profile share codes)
Quick architecture, because it explains the install options later. There are three pieces. The browser is the dashboard, and it does more than you'd think: the propagation engine runs in your browser as WebAssembly, so predictions are computed on your machine. The server is the part that talks to the cluster, the Reverse Beacon Network, POTA, NOAA, NASA, the satellite databases, and caches all of it so a thousand users don't make a thousand requests. That server is either openhamclock.com or your own box. And Rig Bridge is a small optional program that sits on the PC next to your radio and handles CAT control, WSJT-X, your rotator, an APRS TNC, and soon hardware sensors. Two things to know: the hosted site runs our own DX cluster node now, so we're not at the mercy of public nodes. And there are no accounts and no tracking. Your settings live in your browser; you can sync them or share them with a code if you want.
Getting started

Ninety seconds from "what's this?" to operating.

  1. Open openhamclock.com
  2. Enter your callsign and grid (or "use my location")
  3. Pick a layout — Modern is a fine start
  4. Set a DX target: type a call or click the map
  5. Optional: units, time zone, propagation mode and power

Everything after that is optional. Rig Bridge, alerts, layouts and profiles come later, when you want them.

Getting started is genuinely ninety seconds. Open the site, enter your callsign and grid, or let it use your location. Pick a layout; Modern is a good first one. Set a DX target by typing a call or clicking the map. That's it; you're operating. Units, time zone, your propagation mode and power are a few more clicks when you want them. Everything else in this talk, rig control, alerts, layouts, profiles, is layered on when and if you want it. Nothing is required.

The map

The map is the application. Three ways to look at the world.

  • Live gray line with civil / nautical / astronomical twilight · DE/DX short and long path · 30+ toggleable layers
  • Aurora, D-RAP absorption, MUF, VOACAP heatmap, grid squares, CQ/ITU zones, radar, lightning, earthquakes, wildfires, aircraft, city lights…
  • Click anywhere: distance, bearing, grid, local time, nearest city — set it as DX in one click
The map is the application, and you get three projections. The flat map is the everyday one, with eleven basemaps from dark to satellite imagery to topo. The azimuthal projection is centered on you, so a straight line out from the middle is the true beam heading, which is how a rotator thinks. And the 3D globe puts satellites at their actual orbital altitude with great-circle arcs that look like great circles. On all three: the gray line updates live with the three twilight bands, short and long path to your DX, and more than thirty layers you can switch on: aurora, D-layer absorption, MUF, the VOACAP heatmap, grid squares, zones, weather radar, lightning, earthquakes, wildfires, live aircraft, even NASA's city lights at night. Click anywhere on the map and you get distance, bearing, grid, local time and the nearest city, with a one-click set-as-DX.

The DX cluster has looked the same since 1989, and this is the version you wish you'd had. There's a real mode column. Propagation

Real physics, computed for your station, right now.

  • ITU-R P.533 (the VOACAP model) compiled to WebAssembly and run in your browser
  • Per-band reliability chart DE → DX for 24 hours, with your mode, power and antenna
  • Each mode at its real decode threshold — FT8 at −19 dB, CW at +5, SSB at +10. That's why FT8 bands aren't "closed"
  • World heatmap and MUF map overlays; live ionosonde readings from GIRO
  • Band Conditions and Band Health; band-opening alerts toward your continent or zone
  • Solar: SFI, SSN, K/A, X-ray flux, solar wind, Bz, proton flux, NASA SDO imagery, SWPC alerts
  • NCDXF/IBP beacon schedule with a listening log
Propagation. Most tools you've used are effectively screenshots of somebody else's prediction. OpenHamClock runs the ITU-R P.533 engine, the physics VOACAP is built on, compiled to WebAssembly, in your browser, for your station. The chart shows per-band reliability from you to your DX across the day, with your mode, power and antenna. And it runs each mode at its real decode threshold: FT8 at minus nineteen dB, CW at plus five, SSB at plus ten. That's why FT8 bands here are open when other tools say closed. Real example: Bucks County to Tokyo, fifteen meters, late morning: zero percent on SSB, about sixty on FT8. That matches what you hear. There's a world heatmap, a MUF map, live ionosonde readings, band conditions, and band-opening alerts that tell you when the spots say a band has opened toward Europe or Asia from here. The solar side has everything: SFI, sunspots, K and A, X-ray flux so you see the flare before the band drops, solar wind and Bz, live NASA imagery, and the SWPC alert feed. And the panel on the right, Prediction Check, is new this week: it puts the model's prediction for your DE-to-DX path next to what's actually being spotted on that path, and tells you in plain words whether the band is open, marginal or closed.

Rig control

Click a spot. Your radio tunes.

Rig Bridge talks to

  • Yaesu, Kenwood, Elecraft, Icom over USB CAT — no hamlib needed
  • rigctld (Hamlib) · flrig · TCI · FlexRadio SmartSDR · RTL-SDR
  • Rotators via rotctld or PstRotator — bearing line on the map
  • WSJT-X, JTDX, MSHV, JS8Call — decodes on the map, reply from the dashboard
  • APRS TNC (Direwolf / KISS), Winlink, MeshCom
  • Cloud relay: control the shack from anywhere

Band plan overlay on the frequency display warns you off privileges you don't have. Auto-mode picks the right mode for the segment.

Rig control. Click a spot and your radio tunes, with the right mode, while the other guy is still spinning his VFO. That's Rig Bridge, a small program on the PC next to the radio. It speaks directly to Yaesu, Kenwood, Elecraft and Icom over USB, no hamlib needed, and also rigctld, flrig, TCI, the Flex over SmartSDR, and an RTL-SDR for receive. It drives rotators, and draws your beam heading on the map. WSJT-X and friends stream their decodes onto the map, and you can reply from the dashboard. It handles an APRS TNC, Winlink, and MeshCom. And the cloud relay lets you run the shack from a hotel room. One detail people like: the band plan is overlaid on the frequency display, so it warns you when you're about to transmit where your license class doesn't allow.

Install

Pick your poison.

Zero install

Visit openhamclock.com — callsign + grid, done in 30 seconds. Install it as an app from the browser menu.

Raspberry Pi kiosk (Pi 3/4/5, Pi 400)

curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/accius/openhamclock/main/scripts/setup-pi.sh | bash -s -- --kiosk

Fullscreen Chromium on boot. A $70 Pi 400 runs it comfortably.

Docker

docker compose up -d

Self-hosted on your LAN or home server; image on GHCR. Every upstream call goes through your backend.

Mac / Linux / Windows

git clone https://github.com/accius/openhamclock.git
cd openhamclock && npm ci && npm start

Open http://localhost:3000. Electron desktop builds and a Windows one-line installer too.

How do you get it? Four ways, in order of patience. Zero install: openhamclock.com, and you can install it as an app from the browser menu. The Raspberry Pi kiosk: one command and it reboots into a fullscreen wall display; a seventy-dollar Pi 400 runs it comfortably. Docker on a home server. Or clone and run on a Mac, Linux or Windows box; there's an Electron desktop build and a one-line Windows installer. If you self-host, every upstream call goes through your own backend. Nothing about your operating goes anywhere you don't control.

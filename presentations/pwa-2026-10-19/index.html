What it is

One screen for what's happening on the air

OpenHamClock is a free, open-source dashboard that runs in a web browser. It pulls dozens of public data sources onto one map centered on your station:

  • Space weather from NOAA and live images of the sun from NASA
  • Who's being heard right now: the DX cluster and PSKReporter
  • Parks, summits, DXpeditions and contests
  • A propagation prediction calculated for your location

No account, no download, no ads. Your settings stay in your own browser.

In one sentence: OpenHamClock is a free dashboard in your web browser that puts what's happening on the air on one screen. Space weather from NOAA, live images of the sun from NASA, who's being heard right now from the DX cluster and PSKReporter, parks and summits on the air, DXpeditions, contests, and a propagation prediction calculated for your own location. Same idea as the original HamClock you've seen on Raspberry Pis, built from scratch for the web. No account, nothing to install, and your settings live in your browser. The screenshots tonight were taken this morning with the club call, W3SK, in FN20, so the live numbers tonight will be different.

1 · Header

The space-weather strip

What it is How to read it
SFI Solar Flux Index: the sun's radio output at 10.7 cm, a stand-in for how hard it's ionizing the upper atmosphere Higher is better for HF, especially 15–10 m. About 70 is a quiet sun; 120+ good; 150+ excellent.
K Planetary K-index: how disturbed Earth's magnetic field is, 0–9, over 3 hours Low is better. 0–2 quiet. 4 and up turns red: absorption, noise, aurora.
SSN Sunspot number Rises and falls with SFI over the 11-year solar cycle.
A A-index: the whole day's geomagnetic disturbance in one number Under 10 green, 10–19 amber, 20+ red.
SW Solar wind speed, km/s Under 500 green, 500–699 amber, 700+ red. Fast wind stirs up the field.
WORD Geomagnetic field summary from N0NBH (HamQSL) QUIET is green. ACTIVE or any STORM is red.

Rule of thumb: high SFI and low K is a good HF day. This morning: SFI 137, K 0.33. Good.

The right half of the header is the space-weather strip, and this is the slide to take home. SFI, the solar flux index, is the sun's radio output at 10.7 centimeters. It stands in for how strongly the sun is ionizing the upper atmosphere, and more ionization opens the higher bands: 15, 12 and 10 meters. Around 70 is a quiet sun, 120 and up is good, 150 and up is excellent. K is the planetary K-index, 0 to 9, measuring how disturbed Earth's magnetic field is over the last three hours. Here low is good; at 4 and up the app turns it red, because a disturbed field means more absorption and noise, and, for the VHF crowd, aurora. SSN, the sunspot number, tracks SFI over the eleven-year cycle. A is the A-index, the whole day's disturbance in one number. SW is solar wind speed in kilometers per second; fast wind is what stirs up the field. The word at the end is the geomagnetic summary from N0NBH's HamQSL service; this morning it said INACTIVE. One thing to remember: high SFI and low K is a good HF day. This morning was 137 and a third.
2 · DE: your station

"DE" means this end

  • FN20nc is your Maidenhead grid square. FN is a 20°×10° field, 20 is a 2°×1° square, and "nc" narrows it to a few miles. Most of Lower Bucks is FN20.
  • Latitude and longitude: every distance and heading is measured from here.
  • 07:14 → 18:14: your sunrise and sunset, local time. The low bands improve after dark, and gray-line openings happen around these two times.
  • Local weather. The arrow opens wind, humidity, pressure and alerts.
On the left, the first pane is DE, old telegraph shorthand for "from", as in "W3SK de K0CJH". It means your end of the contact. The big amber code is your Maidenhead grid square, which works in three steps: two letters for a field twenty degrees by ten, two digits for a square two degrees by one, and two more letters to narrow it to a few miles. Contesters and VHF operators exchange the four-character version; most of us are FN20. Under it, latitude and longitude, which every distance and heading on the screen is measured from. Then sunrise and sunset in local time. Those matter: the low bands come alive after dark, and the gray line passes over you right around those two times. And your local weather, with more detail behind the arrow.

Reading the map

  • ☀ marks where the sun is directly overhead; the moon icon, where the moon is.
  • Each line is a spot: from the station that heard to the station heard, colored by band. Hover one for details.
  • The band bar at the bottom filters the map to one band.
  • The ticker runs DX news headlines.

The map is the middle of everything. The shaded half is night, and it moves through the day. The boundary is the gray line: twilight, where the D layer that soaks up low-band signals in daytime has faded on one side and not yet built up on the other. Signals along it, especially on 160, 80 and 40, can go remarkably far. The sun symbol marks where the sun is directly overhead, and the moon likewise. Those colors are used everywhere, so a green frequency in the cluster list is 20 meters. Click a band on the bar at the bottom to show only that band. The ticker is DX news. Three ways to draw the world

The buttons at the top of the map switch projections. Flat is the everyday view, and the drop-down next to it picks the basemap: dark, satellite, terrain and so on. Below DX on the left is the Solar pane, with four views; the small button at top right flips between them and the play button rotates them. 3 · Solar · X-ray view

Flares and sudden blackouts

Satellites measure the sun's X-rays. Flares are graded by peak strength, and each class is 10× the one before:

B → C → M → X

  • B and C: background, little or no effect
  • M and X: an HF radio blackout on the sunlit side of Earth, starting within minutes and lasting minutes to hours
Bands go dead in the middle of the day? Check this view first. If X-ray just spiked, it isn't your radio.
The X-ray view is the one to check when things go wrong. Satellites watch the sun's X-ray output, and flares are graded B, C, M and X, each ten times stronger than the last. B and C are background. An M or X flare ionizes the lowest layer of the atmosphere on the daylit side of Earth so strongly that it absorbs HF, and the bands go quiet within minutes. That's a radio blackout, and it lasts from minutes to a couple of hours. So if you're on 20 meters in the afternoon and everything vanishes, look here before you start checking coax. This morning was B5 with a peak around C2: nothing to worry about.
4 · Propagation

Which bands, to there, and when

  • A prediction for the path from your DE to your DX, not a general forecast
  • Rows are bands, columns are the 24 hours (UTC). The white box is now.
  • Color is reliability: green likely, yellow/orange possible, red/dark unlikely.
  • Uses the ITU's HF model (ITU-R P.533, the physics behind VOACAP), calculated in your browser with your mode, power and antenna (default SSB, 100 W).
Reading it: FN20 → Tokyo, SSB. 40 and 30 m are best roughly 08–14 UTC (our night). 17 and 15 m have an evening window around 21–00 UTC. 10 m: no.
The bottom pane on the left is the propagation prediction, and the key point is that it's for a specific path: from your station to whatever DX target you've set. Rows are bands, columns are the hours of the day in UTC, and the white box is right now. Green means the signal will probably get through on most days; red and dark mean probably not. It runs the ITU's HF propagation model, the same physics VOACAP is built on, in your browser with today's solar numbers. Reading it for Tokyo on SSB at 100 watts: 40 and 30 meters are the best bet from roughly 08 to 14 UTC, our overnight. Then there's an evening window on 17 and 15 meters around 21 to 00 UTC, which is 5 to 8 PM here. So if you want Japan on SSB tonight, try 17 or 15 around dinner time.
4 · Propagation

MUF, LUF, and the mode you're using

  • MUF, maximum usable frequency: the highest frequency the ionosphere will typically bend back to earth on this path right now. Much above it, the signal goes into space.
  • LUF, lowest usable frequency: below it, daytime absorption eats the signal.
  • Bands between the two can work; signal strength decides whether they do. Right now, only 20 and 30 m show any chance.
  • Bars view: the same prediction for this hour only, best band first.
Mode matters. FT8 can be decoded far below the noise; SSB needs a signal well above it. Switch the mode drop-down to FT8 and bands that are "closed" for SSB often open up.
Two numbers at the top of the propagation pane are worth learning. MUF, maximum usable frequency, is the highest frequency the ionosphere will typically reflect back to earth on this path right now; go well above it and your signal heads off into space. It's a median, not a hard wall, which is why 20 meters at 14 megahertz still shows a 22 percent chance against an 11 megahertz MUF. LUF, lowest usable frequency, is the floor: below it, the daytime D layer absorbs the signal. Bands between the two can work, and whether they actually do depends on how much signal arrives. That's why 40 meters shows zero at midday even though it's above the LUF. The bars view is the same thing for the current hour, best first. The drop-downs set your mode, power and antenna, and mode makes a big difference: FT8 decodes well below the noise, SSB needs a signal comfortably above it. Switch to FT8 and you'll often see bands open that were "closed" for SSB, which matches what you see on the waterfall.

General band conditions

  • From N0NBH's widely used HamQSL report: each band rated GOOD / FAIR / POOR, for day and for night
  • VHF conditions: aurora and sporadic-E openings on 6 m and up
  • Noise: expected background noise, in S-units
General vs. your path. This morning 15 m was rated GOOD overall, while the prediction for FN20 → Tokyo on SSB said 0% at that hour. Both are right: one is about the band, the other is about your signal to that place.
Flip the propagation pane once more and you get general band conditions, from N0NBH's HamQSL report that you've probably seen on QRZ pages. Each band gets good, fair or poor, for day and for night, plus VHF notes for aurora and sporadic E, and the expected noise level. A useful lesson from this morning: the general report said 15 meters was GOOD, and the path prediction to Tokyo said zero percent at that hour. They don't contradict each other. The general report means the band is open somewhere; the path prediction is about whether your signal reaches that particular place. Use the general one to decide whether to turn the radio on, and the path one to decide where to point.
6 · DX cluster

Who's being heard, right now

A spot is a report that a station was heard on a frequency. Spots come from other hams and from automatic receivers (the Reverse Beacon Network, RBN), shared over the worldwide DX cluster network.

Each row: frequency (colored by band) · callsign · mode · de the spotter · how long ago

  • Click a row to make it your DX target and show it on the map.
  • 🎧 listens on a nearby web receiver (KiwiSDR), already tuned to it.
  • "50/162": 50 rows shown of 162 spots received. Search and filters narrow it.

With your log imported: NEW new country · BAND new on this band · DUPE already worked · ✓ worked on another band or mode

Over to the right side. The DX cluster goes back to the packet radio days: a network of nodes where hams post "I just heard this station on this frequency." Today most spots come from the Reverse Beacon Network, automatic receivers that decode CW and digital signals and post them, which is why there are so many CW and FT8 spots. Each row is the frequency colored by band, the callsign, the mode, who spotted it, and how long ago. Click a row and it becomes your DX target and appears on the map. The headphones open a KiwiSDR, a web-based receiver, near the station and tuned to that frequency, so you can hear it from a laptop. "50 of 162" means 50 rows shown out of 162 spots received; the search and filter buttons narrow it by band, mode or continent. And if you import your log, spots get tagged: NEW for a country you've never worked, BAND for one you need on this band, DUPE if you've already worked them here.
7 · PSKReporter

Who hears you

Digital-mode software (WSJT-X, JTDX and others) uploads every station it decodes to PSKReporter.info. This pane pulls the reports involving your callsign.

  • ▲ Heard: stations that decoded your signal
  • ▼ Hearing: stations you decoded
  • Numbers are signal-to-noise in dB. A gold ★ means you hear each other: a contact is likely.
  • The drop-down sets how many minutes of reports to keep.
"Waiting for spots… (TX to see reports)" just means you haven't transmitted yet. Call CQ on FT8 for a few minutes and watch the map: a free, honest test of your antenna.
Next is PSKReporter. When you run FT8 or other digital modes, your software reports every station it decodes to a website called PSKReporter, and so does everyone else's. This pane pulls out the reports involving your callsign. Heard is everyone who decoded you, Hearing is everyone you decoded, each with a signal-to-noise figure, and they appear on the map. A gold star means you're hearing each other, which is when a contact is likely. "Waiting for spots" is normal: nobody can report hearing you until you transmit. Call CQ on FT8 for five minutes and watch the map fill in. That's the cheapest, most honest antenna test there is. The WSJT-X tab shows decodes from your own copy of WSJT-X, if you connect it.

Below that are the on-the-air programs, one tab each. POTA, Parks on the Air, is the one most of you know: operators set up portable in a park, and everyone else hunts them. SOTA is the same from a mountain summit, WWFF is nature reserves, and WWBOTA is historic bunkers. Activators are usually running low power and want contacts, so this is one of the friendliest places on the bands to find someone to work.

Putting it together

Ten seconds before you turn the radio on

  1. Header: SFI up and K low? High flux and a quiet field: the higher bands are worth a look.
  2. X-ray: any M or X flare? If the day side is blacked out, wait, or use the night-side bands.
  3. Propagation: set a DX target and read the chart Which band, and what time, for the place you want.
  4. Map and cluster: is anyone actually getting through? Lines on the map are proof. Filter to the band you picked.
  5. Listen: 🎧 on a spot, then tune the radio Parks and summits are the easiest contacts on any given day.
Here's how it fits together as a ten-second routine before you sit down to operate. Glance at the header: is the flux up and the K low? Check the X-ray view: any big flare? If so, the daylight side is blacked out for a while. Set a DX target, wherever you want to work, and read the propagation chart for which band and what time. Then check the map and the cluster for proof: predictions are predictions, but lines on the map are people actually getting through. Filter to your band. Then listen, on the web SDR with the headphones button or on your own radio. And if you just want contacts, the POTA tab is the easiest place to find them.
